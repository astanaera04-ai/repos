Patient Zero

A virus is spreading and you are the analyst. This console runs on your own data. Every picture below is drawn by the lab itself; the tick next to each mission tells whether your program agrees with the lab. The lab never shows you the answer: it shows you how the algorithm gets there.

Case map your results on the reference genome

Red ticks: the places where your KMP found the marker. Move the mouse over a tick.

 

1. Mutations

How many single-letter changes turn strain A (old) into strain B (new)?

Hover over the map.
Move along the alignment

What you are looking at. Every cell of the map is one question: how far apart are the first i letters of A and the first j letters of B? Dark is close, bright is far. Your table is filled from the corner, and the cheapest route from corner to corner (the white line) is the list of mutations. A diagonal step is a letter that stayed or was replaced, a step down is a deletion, a step to the right is an insertion. The strip on the right is that route written out as letters.

2. Shared core

Which letters do the two strains still have in common, in the same order?

What you are looking at. The top bar is strain A, the bottom bar is strain B. Every line connects a letter that belongs to the longest common subsequence. Parallel lines mean a piece that was copied without change, lines that lean show that letters were added or removed before this place. Red notches on a bar are letters that are not in the common core: they are the changes.

3. The marker

Where does the dangerous gene sit in the genome? Watch KMP read the text once, without ever going back.

What you are looking at. The top line is the text, the line below is the marker slid along it. The table under the marker is its prefix function. When a letter does not fit, KMP does not start again: the table says how much of the match is still useful (the number in the cell before the mismatch), the marker jumps by that and the text pointer stays.

4. Copied words

Which words of 12 letters of strain A also appear in strain B? Slide a window and keep a number that you update in one step.

Hash sizeStart in A at

What you are looking at. The hash of a window is a number made from its letters. When the window moves one letter to the right, the old letter is subtracted, the rest is shifted and the new letter is added: one step instead of twelve. If the hash of the window is among the hashes of B, the words are probably equal, but only the letters can say it for sure. With a tiny hash size many different words share a number: switch it on and see how many false alarms appear.

5. Whom to test

Every infection link needs at least one tested person. Watch the numbers climb from the leaves to the root.

tested by your programchosen by the labt/s = cheapest cost if the person is tested / not tested

What you are looking at. Each person gets two numbers, looking only at the people below them: the cheapest cost if this person is tested, and if not. If the person is not tested, every child must be. If the person is tested, each child may be either way. The numbers climb to the root and the answer is read from the root; then the choice is rebuilt from the top. Equal prices can give several equally cheap sets, so your red people may differ from the lab's rings; the cost must be the same.
